Physics calculations become easier when you write down what each number means before choosing an equation. This original example assumes **constant acceleration**.

## The question

A cyclist travels in a straight line. Her speed increases steadily from **6 m/s** to **18 m/s** in **4 s**. Find her acceleration and the distance travelled in those four seconds.

## Step 1: Find the acceleration

Acceleration is the change in velocity divided by time:

```text
acceleration = (final velocity − initial velocity) ÷ time
             = (18 − 6) ÷ 4
             = 3 m/s²
```

The answer is **3 m/s²**. The squared unit belongs to acceleration because velocity changes by 3 metres per second **each second**.

## Step 2: Find the distance

Because the acceleration is constant, the average velocity is halfway between the initial and final velocities:

```text
average velocity = (6 + 18) ÷ 2 = 12 m/s
distance = average velocity × time = 12 × 4 = 48 m
```

The cyclist travels **48 m**. You can also picture a velocity–time graph: its area is a rectangle (`6 × 4 = 24 m`) plus a triangle (`½ × 4 × 12 = 24 m`). Together they give 48 m.

### The easy mistake

Using the final speed for all four seconds would give `18 × 4 = 72 m`. That assumes the cyclist was moving at 18 m/s from the start, even though she began at 6 m/s.

## Your turn

A runner speeds up uniformly from **3 m/s** to **15 m/s** in **6 s**. Find the acceleration and distance.

**Check:** acceleration is `(15 − 3) ÷ 6 = 2 m/s²`. Average velocity is `(3 + 15) ÷ 2 = 9 m/s`, so distance is `9 × 6 = 54 m`.
